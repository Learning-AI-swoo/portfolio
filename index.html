How I Think

Three principles

Remove logistical friction, not intellectual friction

There are two kinds of struggle in learning. One is productive: a student wrestling with a complex clinical concept, building understanding through effort. That's the learning. You don't remove that. The other is just friction. Confusing instructions, clunky navigation, unclear rubrics. That's wasted energy with no learning value.

Technology should eliminate the second kind so learners can focus on the first. That's the logic behind everything I build.

An AI chatbot for async courses so students get answers at 2am. Clinical readiness modules so interns aren't anxious about protocols before day one. Online learning readiness resources so students struggle with content, not the platform.

What's the actual problem?

Technology is often the right answer, but only when it's actually the problem. When the root cause is structural, a technical solution just papers over it.

A real example

We have multiple campuses, each with its own culture and community profile. At one campus, there was a language barrier in clinical internships. Many faculty and patients shared a primary language that some interns didn't speak. The initial push was to find a HIPAA-compliant medical translator tool. I did the market research. We were ready to implement.

But it didn't address the root cause. The problem wasn't translation. We didn't have enough linguistically diverse faculty or a broad enough patient base at that site. A translation app doesn't fix a community gap. The real solution was hiring and outreach. That's working. Slowly, but it's addressing the actual problem.

Now I always ask: what's the actual problem here? Is technology the right solution, or are we treating symptoms?

Build capacity, not dependency

My job isn't just solving problems. It's helping people grow confident enough to solve them themselves. Templates, tutorials, support sessions. The goal is always self-sufficiency. With students, I don't teach which buttons to click. I teach how to evaluate tools critically, so when new ones emerge, they can figure it out.

Case Study 01

Building AI Literacy from Zero

AI Integration & Educational Innovation — From institutional policy to faculty readiness to student competency, I addressed all three layers.

Problem

When AI took off, VUIM was in the same boat as every other school. Students were already using it. Faculty weren't sure what to do. No policy, no guidance, no structured way to build competency.

Approach

Step one was working with the curriculum committee to create an institutional AI policy. Simple bottom line: AI shouldn't replace learning. Using AI is like getting help from a colleague: you give credit, you disclose it.

But policy alone doesn't build skills. For healthcare students especially, the real risk isn't that they'll use AI. It's that they'll use it without critical thinking. They'll take a ChatGPT output about a diagnosis at face value.

So I designed a 10-week blended AI Literacy course built around Anthropic's 4D Framework (Discover, Discern, Design, Delegate). 6 synchronous Zoom sessions alternating with 4 async weeks:

  • Critical evaluation: how to assess whether an AI output is reliable
  • Red team testing: students deliberately try to break AI to understand its limits
  • Prompt engineering as a thinking skill, not a trick
  • Ethics deep dive: responsible use, data privacy, detecting bias
  • Capstone: students build their own custom AI assistant

Pre/post surveys aligned to Kirkpatrick Levels 1–3 for evaluation.

Beyond the course, I built AI tools that directly support student learning. The Intake Interview Simulator: I worked with faculty to get the clinical content (TCM diagnostic principles, interview protocols, charting standards) and designed a system prompt so AI generates a realistic patient profile and role-plays as that patient. Students practice conducting intake interviews anytime, and the system evaluates whether they asked the right diagnostic questions, followed proper sequencing, gathered key information. Like a standardized patient encounter, available 24/7.

I also built a course-specific AI chatbot for a fully async Western Medicine foundations course. Transcribed lecture audio with Whisper, structured it as a knowledge base, wrote system instructions with clear constraints: help students understand concepts, but don't give away quiz answers. Support learning, don't replace it.

I also founded the VUIM AI Lab: a monthly peer learning community for faculty and staff. Voluntary participation, psychological safety, low-stakes environment. And I currently lead the Education Resources subcommittee of the NCCAOM AI Taskforce, shaping AI competency standards for 33,000+ professionals nationally.

Result

VUIM went from zero AI infrastructure to having institutional policy, a faculty community of practice, a student-facing course, AI-powered learning tools, and national-level thought leadership. All in under two years.

Honest note

The chatbot launched as a beta with a small group. Students liked having 24/7 support that actually knew the course content. But we couldn't scale it. No IT team, no budget for a standalone wrapper. Custom GPT has limitations. Both tools are modest. I'm not pretending I built an enterprise platform. But they solved real problems with the resources we had. The design thinking transfers regardless of the technology budget. Understanding the pedagogy, setting the right constraints, keeping humans in the loop: that's what matters.

Case Study 02

Building Distance Education Infrastructure

Program Architecture & Compliance — 150+ courses, 3 campuses, 5 accreditation bodies. Here's how the foundation was built.

Problem

When I joined VUIM during COVID, the school was operating under emergency exemptions for online learning. We knew that was going away. We needed to serve students across multiple states and offer coursework online. But we had zero infrastructure for distance education. No policies, no faculty training, no evaluation systems. Nothing.

Approach

I studied the C-RAC Distance Education guidelines and federal Regular and Substantive Interaction requirements. Did a full gap analysis. And I was realistic. I looked at what resources we actually had, not what we wished we had.

The key insight: I couldn't just write policies and expect compliance. Faculty had no support system. So I took a phased approach.

Step one: I proposed and established the CTLE — Center for Teaching & Learning Excellence. Before we could do distance ed well, we needed a home base for faculty development.

From there, layer by layer: RSI training, pedagogy workshops, instructional design support, a management and monitoring system, DE-specific course evaluation criteria.

Result

VUIM now has a functioning distance education program compliant with federal and state requirements across three states. 150+ courses managed quarterly. 5 accreditation bodies maintained in compliance (NC-SARA, ACAHM, three state certifications). Institutional assessment framework built from scratch.

Honest note

It wasn't glamorous work. A lot of it was reading regulations and translating them into practical steps. But that's how you build something that lasts.

I also learned a hard lesson early on. Before CTLE, I tried to launch a comprehensive faculty development program. Multiple modules, robust assessment. It flopped. Participation was minimal. I'd designed in a vacuum. I assumed my expertise meant I knew best. I never asked faculty what problems they actually wanted solved. When I later built the CTLE, I started with their pain points: LMS confusion, grading challenges, technology frustration. The adoption was completely different.

Case Study 03

Data Infrastructure from Nothing

Learning Analytics & Data-Informed Decisions — You can't support teaching and learning if you don't know what's happening.

Problem

When I joined VUIM, I started asking basic questions. How many patients do our interns see per term? What do board exam trends look like? What does the data say about curriculum decisions?

Nobody knew. Not "didn't have the report handy" — nobody had ever collected or analyzed this data. They didn't know where to download it from the EHR. Learning data was the same: graduation exams, phase exams, board results existed, but nobody was tracking any of it. Decisions were based on anecdotal information. "I feel like students are struggling with this" became the basis for curriculum changes.

Approach

I established the Center for Learning Analytics. Nobody told me to. There was no budget for it. But the gap was clear.

I taught myself what I needed. I knew basic Python and Excel when I started. Learned Power BI from scratch. Later used AI to improve my code, though I never fed actual student or patient data to external AI tools, just the code itself.

I also built a local transcription pipeline using OpenAI's Whisper, entirely on-premises. Most paid transcription services are wrappers around the same open-source model. Our clinical content sometimes references patient scenarios, so I didn't want data going to third-party servers. Local processing: zero recurring cost, no file size limits, no data leaving the institution.

Result

We now have what I call the VUIM datahouse. Pipelines for clinical data, learning outcomes, board exam tracking. Quarterly, monthly, yearly reports, all with interactive Power BI dashboards. The clinical data alone: hundreds of thousands of data points. Patient visits, ICD-10 codes, supervisor utilization, demographics. Before, this was invisible. Now it drives strategy.

95% reduction in data processing and reporting time — from days to hours.

Honest note

I'm not a data wizard. The point is: I saw what was needed to support teaching and learning, and I built it with whatever resources I had.
