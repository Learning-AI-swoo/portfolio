How I Think

Three principles

Remove logistical friction, not intellectual friction

There are two kinds of struggle in learning. One is productive: a student wrestling with a complex clinical concept, building real understanding through effort. That's the learning. You don't remove that. The other kind is just friction: confusing instructions, clunky navigation, unclear rubrics. That's wasted energy with no learning value. Technology should eliminate the second kind so learners can focus on the first.

That's the logic behind everything I build. An AI chatbot for Western Medicine async courses so students can get answers at 2am without waiting on an instructor. Clinical training readiness modules so interns aren't anxious about procedure protocols before their first day. Online learning readiness resources so students aren't struggling with the platform when they should be struggling with the content. The technology handles the logistics. The learning handles itself.

What's the actual problem?

Technology is often the right answer, but only when it's actually the problem. When the root cause is structural — a staffing gap, a broken process, a culture issue — a technical solution just papers over it. I've seen requests for new tools where the real fix was a hiring decision, a clearer policy, or better communication. Identifying which situation you're in before reaching for a solution is what separates good design from expensive noise.

Build capacity, not dependency

My job isn't just solving problems. It's helping people grow confident enough to solve them. Templates, tutorials, support sessions: the goal is always self-sufficiency, not quick fixes. With students, I don't teach which buttons to click. I teach how to evaluate tools critically, so when new ones emerge, they can figure it out. The aim is empowerment: people who walk away more capable, not more dependent on me to keep things running. Everything I design is oriented toward that kind of ownership.

AI Integration & Educational Innovation

Pioneering AI in healthcare education

When AI tools became ubiquitous, our institution had the same gap most did: students were already using these tools, but there was no policy, no guidance, and no structured way to build actual competency. I saw that gap as a design problem with three layers: institutional policy, faculty readiness, and student skill. I addressed all three. From governance frameworks to faculty communities of practice to a course designed to build real, critical AI competency in healthcare students.

AI Literacy for TCM Professionals

Policy alone doesn't build skills. So I designed a 10-week blended course around Anthropic's 4D Framework. Students don't learn to use AI passively. They learn to question it, stress-test it, and decide when not to use it.

6 synchronous Zoom sessions + 4 async weeks. Pre/post surveys aligned to Kirkpatrick Levels 1-3. Key activities: red team testing (deliberately breaking AI), custom assistant builds, personal ethics frameworks.

AI-Augmented Knowledge Work

Information overload is a real problem for anyone doing knowledge work. I built an AI-augmented pipeline that turns raw information intake into a structured second brain, capturing, processing, and surfacing knowledge when it's needed.

The Article Curator is one example: what used to take an hour per article (saving, re-reading, manually summarizing) now takes seconds. Two earlier attempts failed before finding the right architecture, one that keeps human judgment at the center (deciding what's worth saving) and lets AI handle the processing (summarizing, tagging, structuring). HTML form → Make.com → Claude API → Notion + Google Drive. Cost: <$0.01/use.

AI-Enhanced Learning Content at Scale

Developing quality learning content is resource-intensive across the board: time, expertise, production effort. Working with AI, I've been able to keep SME input and learning objectives intact while running a much leaner development process.

A local Whisper transcription pipeline converts clinical lecture audio into structured knowledge assets, entirely on-premises. Cost: zero. Data stays in-house, which matters when content references patient scenarios. Most paid transcription services are wrappers around the same open-source tool. Understanding what's under the hood is part of the job.
