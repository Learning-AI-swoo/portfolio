Case Study 01

Built the infrastructure for scalable distance education and online doctoral delivery

The institution needed a distance education system that could scale across campuses, support a fully online professional doctoral program, and hold up under accreditation and state authorization requirements. I approached that challenge as an infrastructure problem rather than a course-by-course design task.

My role

Lead instructional designer, systems builder, and compliance architect.

Scope

Distance education infrastructure, NC-SARA participation and renewal, online doctoral program architecture, faculty implementation support, and accreditation-aligned curriculum mapping.

What I built

Institution-wide online learning structures, reusable compliance processes, program architecture, aligned learning models, and a queryable curriculum-mapping database for ongoing accreditation work.

Challenge

The school already had online delivery in place, but it was still largely organized around synchronous Zoom meetings rather than a unified distance education operating structure. At the same time, leadership wanted to grow a fully online professional doctoral program that could recruit nationally. That required more than course design. The institution needed a system that could support scale, faculty implementation, regulatory compliance, and accreditation alignment in a coordinated way.

System I built

After joining the institution, I helped move online delivery from a primarily synchronous, Zoom-based model into a more fully developed distance education system. That included distance education expectations, LMS engagement structures, recurring NC-SARA processes, compliance documentation, course-level quality review processes, faculty-facing guidance, and broader use of flipped, hybrid, asynchronous, and activity-based learning models.

I then redesigned the doctoral pathway inside that structure. I conducted market review, defined four clinical focus areas, aligned program objectives with ACAHM competency domains, mapped course-level outcomes, and designed learning models that could support academic quality in a fully online format.

To make the system more durable, I also structured the competency mapping as a normalized JSON database. That created a reusable data layer for accreditation reporting and made future revision work more manageable.

What changed

The resulting infrastructure now supports more than 600 courses and more than 2,000 annual course enrollments across three campuses. The online doctoral program grew from fewer than 20 students per term to more than 60 after launch. Just as important, the school gained a reusable distance education and compliance structure that could support future growth beyond a single program.

Honest note

I led the system design, program architecture, and compliance structure. Enrollment growth also depended on admissions effort, leadership support, and institutional follow-through. My main contribution was building the underlying structure that made scaling possible.

Case Study 02

Built the institution’s learning analytics and reporting infrastructure

The institution had large amounts of academic and clinical data, but very little of it was organized for decision-making. I approached that gap as an infrastructure problem and built the reporting systems, workflows, and dashboards needed to make the data usable.

My role

Founder of the analytics function and primary builder of the reporting environment.

Scope

Learning analytics, clinic reporting, board exam tracking, dashboard design, and local workflow development.

What I built

Reporting pipelines, dashboard logic, data workflows, and recurring views that turned fragmented records into usable decision support.

Challenge

Basic questions about academic performance, clinic activity, board outcomes, and program trends were difficult to answer because the institution did not yet have an organized analytics structure. Data existed across multiple systems, but it was not being transformed into information people could reliably use for planning, review, or decision-making.

System I built

I established the reporting structure from scratch and taught myself the tools required to make it work. That included Power BI dashboards, clinic data workflows, board exam tracking, and other reporting pipelines that translated scattered records into usable views for academic and operational review.

I also developed local and privacy-aware workflows when cloud tools were not the best fit because of cost, practicality, or data sensitivity. The goal was to build a reporting environment that the institution could reuse over time.

Dashboard screenshots from production environment. Data anonymized for confidentiality.

What changed

The institution now has a working analytics environment for academic and clinical reporting, with dashboards and recurring reports that support planning and decision-making. Processing and reporting time dropped by 95 percent. What once took days of manual work now takes hours, with far greater visibility and consistency.

Honest note

I did not come into this role as a formal data specialist. The work grew from a practical institutional need to make teaching, learning, and operations more visible and more manageable.

Case Study 03

Built an AI adoption system across policy, faculty development, and student learning

As AI began shaping teaching, student work, and institutional decision-making, the organization needed a coordinated response. I approached that challenge as a systems problem and built a capability layer that connected policy, faculty support, and student learning.

My role

Lead designer, strategist, and institutional implementation partner.

Scope

Policy guidance, faculty development, student learning design, AI experimentation, and national competency framework work.

What I built

Institutional guidance, faculty support structures, student-facing AI literacy learning, and early workflow and simulation prototypes that supported more responsible adoption.

Challenge

AI was already affecting classroom practice, student workflow, and faculty judgment, but the institution did not yet have a shared structure for how to respond. Faculty needed practical support. Students needed a way to develop judgment rather than dependence. The organization also needed policy language that could actually guide practice.

System I built

I built the response across multiple layers. First, I helped shape institutional guidance so faculty had a more usable starting point for classroom decisions. Second, I designed faculty development resources and practical support around AI use in teaching and course design. Third, I developed student-facing learning experiences, including a full AI literacy course and early concepts for simulation-based tools.

This work later extended beyond the institution. Through the NCBAHM AI Taskforce, I contributed substantial design work to national AI competency framework development intended to support the profession more broadly.

What changed

The work gave the institution a more structured way to respond to AI across governance, faculty support, student learning, and early experimentation. It created a practical foundation for guided adoption inside the institution, while also connecting that work to broader field-level competency framework development.

Honest note

Some of the student-facing tools are still early prototypes. The main value came from building a coherent foundation first, then expanding where the institution had real readiness and capacity.

Technical builds and API integration

Healing Walk: API-driven interactive narrative

Built and deployed a bilingual (Korean/English) interactive narrative web app that uses the Claude API to generate sensory, choice-driven walking experiences. Users choose paths through five nature ambiences; the AI generates narration, controls scene transitions, and produces a reflective diary entry at the end.

Single-file vanilla HTML/JS (no framework, no build step). Integrates Claude API messages endpoint with a structured system prompt (v3) that controls narrative style, choice formatting, and turn limits. Response parser extracts five tag types from each API response to drive scene images, ambient sound, particle effects, and surprise events. Howler.js handles audio crossfade. Full i18n system switches UI text, fonts, and system prompts by language.

Designed the prompt engineering to enforce output structure while preserving literary quality, and built client-side guardrails (22-turn hard limit on both client and server) to handle cases where the model drifts from format instructions.

Syllabus analysis automation with Claude Code

Used Claude Code to extract textbook information from 444 syllabi files, deduplicate entries across varied citation formats, and cross-reference results against NCCAOM board exam content outlines. The output: a master spreadsheet with titles, authors, ISBNs, source syllabi, and board exam relevance flags.

Claude Code autonomously planned the task, wrote and executed Python scripts, parsed .docx files with regex-based section detection, and handled multiple citation styles (author-first, title-first, mixed). The main challenge was deduplication. Identical textbooks appeared in different formats across syllabi, and the initial prompts missed many matches.

Solved this through four iterations of prompt refinement: from single-pass extraction (v1) to fuzzy matching with thresholds (v2) to a two-step extract-then-dedup pipeline (v3) to a subject-specific subfolder approach with semantic name detection (v4). The final version produced accurate results. Total time: about 30 minutes, versus an estimated two weeks manually.

How I Work

How I work

Protect the friction that helps learning

I pay close attention to the difference between productive effort and avoidable friction. Good learning often requires challenge, repetition, uncertainty, and real cognitive work. I do not try to remove that.

What I try to remove is the friction that does not help. Unclear instructions, weak course structure, scattered information, clumsy workflows, and poorly designed handoffs can drain time and energy without improving learning or performance.

This idea shapes how I design courses, support faculty, structure training, and build systems. The goal is to make room for the effort that matters.

Define the real problem before choosing a tool

I do not assume that every problem needs a technical answer. Some problems are structural. Some are instructional. Some come from policy, culture, or coordination gaps. The tool only helps once the real problem is clear.

That usually means slowing down at the beginning, asking what is actually breaking down, and choosing a response that fits the situation rather than forcing a preferred solution.

Build for reuse, clarity, and capacity

I prefer systems that keep helping after the first implementation. That can mean reusable templates, clearer logic, better documentation, stronger training, or workflows that other people can actually sustain.

The goal is not only to solve the immediate problem. It is to leave the organization better able to handle similar problems the next time.
